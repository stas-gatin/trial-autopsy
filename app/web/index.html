The problem

A failed heart-drug trial costs hundreds of millions of dollars, and the reason often stays unknown for years.

This page explains, without jargon, why that happens and what Trial Autopsy does about it.

1

How a new heart drug is tested

Before a drug reaches patients it goes through three stages of human trials. The last one, phase 3, is the big one: thousands of patients, several years, and costs from tens to hundreds of millions of dollars. It answers one question: does the drug actually prevent heart attacks, strokes and deaths?

The most common reason a late-stage drug fails is simple: it does not work well enough. In one analysis of drugs that failed in late development, 57% failed for lack of efficacy.1

2

When a trial fails, people need an answer the same day

On 4 September 2026 Novartis announced that its Lp(a) drug pelacarsen missed its goal in a phase 3 trial of 8,323 patients.2 Within days, Amgen shares fell about 10% (8 September), as reported in the financial press3. Amgen had not failed. It owns a different drug against the same target. Analysts asked whether the drug, the trial or the whole idea was wrong. Nobody could say.

3

Why the answer takes years

Trials are designed to answer whether a drug works, not why it did not. The public trial registry usually records the reason for stopping in a few words:

ClinicalTrials.gov · “Why stopped”
“Due to futility”

We checked 500 stopped phase 3 trials: the median explanation is 7 words, and only 9 of 500 mention anything about the biology.4 The real explanation usually comes from studies published years later. Two cholesterol drugs of the CETP class failed in 2012 and 2015. The genetic study that explained why appeared in 2017.5

4

The key insight: nature has already run the experiment

Some people are born with natural gene variants that switch a protein down for life, much as a drug would. If those people have fewer heart attacks, a drug that blocks the same protein should help too. If they don't, the drug is unlikely to work. Scientists call this Mendelian randomization.

Born with the protein switched downfewer heart attacks? → the target is real
Born with the normal genethe comparison group

Drugs whose target has this kind of human genetic support are about 2.6 times more likely to succeed.6 Trials stopped for lack of efficacy tend to lack such support.7

5

Three common ways a heart drug fails

Wrong target

The protein does not cause the disease

Darapladib blocked the enzyme Lp-PLA2 by about 65% in two trials with 29,000 patients. People born with an inactive version of that enzyme have no fewer heart attacks. No effect was possible.

Wrong biomarker

The drug moves a number that doesn't matter

Dalcetrapib and niacin raised “good” HDL cholesterol. Genetics shows raising HDL itself does not prevent heart attacks. The trials failed.

Effect too small

The drug works a little, but the trial can't see it

Evacetrapib lowered the harmful particles (apoB) by only about 15%, worth roughly a 5–7% benefit. The trial was sized to detect about 14%. It almost had to fail.

6

What a better answer is worth

A large cardiovascular outcome trial enrols 10,000–30,000 patients for 4–6 years and costs hundreds of millions of dollars. The value of a good explanation comes at three moments:

Before phase 3 · the drug's owner

Don't run a trial that cannot succeed

Four CETP-inhibitor outcome trials enrolled 73,479 patients (ILLUMINATE 15,067; dal-OUTCOMES 15,871; ACCELERATE 12,092; REVEAL 30,449).8 Only REVEAL showed a benefit, and a small one. From evidence published before each result, our agents named the cause for dal-OUTCOMES and ACCELERATE: the drug moved a biomarker (HDL) that does not cause heart disease. An undersized trial, like pelacarsen's (about a 50% chance of detecting its expected benefit), can instead be enlarged before it starts, which costs far less than losing it.

One avoided or resized trial: ~$100M–$1B and 4–6 years
Result day · investors and competitors

Know in minutes whether the target or the trial failed

After the pelacarsen news, Amgen, with a different drug on the same target, fell about 10% in days.3 For a company of that size, that is billions of dollars of market value moving on one question: is the Lp(a) hypothesis wrong, or was this trial too small? Trial Autopsy answers it with quotes the same day.

Market value at stake: billions, decided in hours
Every day · analysts

Turn weeks of reading into minutes

A careful manual autopsy means reading the registry, genetics and every relevant paper: days to weeks of expert time, roughly $8,000–16,000 per trial at typical analyst rates. A Trial Autopsy run reads the same sources in about a quarter of an hour, on open NVIDIA Nemotron models, and code checks every quote and number.

Per analysis: weeks and ~$10,000 → about 15 minutes

These are order-of-magnitude estimates, not guarantees. In blind tests on 36 finished trials the agents named the experts' main cause in 26 and called 16 of the 22 outcomes the calculator could size, so Trial Autopsy is an early warning and a second opinion with its evidence attached, not a replacement for experts. But helping avoid a single doomed outcome trial is worth more than running the tool for decades.

7

What Trial Autopsy does

It does in minutes what normally takes experts weeks or years. Its agents collect the registry record, human genetics, published trial data and the company's announcement. They run four fixed checks: target, biomarker, effect size, detectability. Then they write a diagnosis in which every number links to a verbatim quote. Before a trial reads out, the same engine gives a forecast: the chance of success and the weakest link.
