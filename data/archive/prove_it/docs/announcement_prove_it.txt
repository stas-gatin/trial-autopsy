Bristol-Myers Squibb - Newsroom - Press Release - New Study Demonstrates The Benefits Of Intensive Statin Therapy In Patients Hospitalized With Acute Coronary Syndrome Press Releases Other News Press Archives » BMS.com Home » Newsroom » Press Release Search News New Study Demonstrates The Benefits Of Intensive Statin Therapy In Patients Hospitalized With Acute Coronary Syndrome PRINCETON, NEW JERSEY (March 8, 2004) -- Results from a new study demonstrate the benefit of initiating intensive cholesterol-lowering therapy within 10 days in people hospitalized with an acute coronary syndrome. The TIMI 22 study (Pravastatin or Atorvastatin Evaluation and Infection Therapy) results were presented today at the American College of Cardiology's 53rd Annual Scientific Session in New Orleans. The study evaluated the role of intensive cholesterol-lowering therapy, defined as high-dose atorvastatin 80 mg vs. standard cholesterol-lowering therapy, defined as standard-dose pravastatin 40 mg, in 4,162 patients hospitalized with acute coronary syndrome whose cholesterol-lowering therapy was initiated within 10 days of their acute event. The primary endpoint of the study was a composite of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, unstable angina requiring hospitalization, revascularization more than 30 days after randomization, or stroke. There was a statistically significant difference (p=0.005) in event rates in the study over two years of follow-up (26.3 percent with pravastatin 40 mg, and 22.4 percent with atorvastatin 80 mg). The benefits of intensive statin treatment were most pronounced in patients hospitalized with acute coronary syndrome with baseline LDL-C (or "bad" cholesterol) levels of 125 mg/dL or higher. Early intervention with intensive statin therapy in this prespecified cohort of study subjects resulted in a rate of occurrence of the primary composite endpoint of 20.1 percent as compared to a rate of 28.2 percent for subjects treated with standard-dose statin therapy. For patients with baseline LDL-C levels of less than 125 mg/dL the event rates were similar in the intensive and standard statin treatment groups (23.5 percent and 25.6 percent, respectively). "These findings are important in helping doctors appropriately treat their acute coronary syndrome patients," said lead investigator Christopher P. Cannon, M.D., Cardiovascular Division at Brigham and Women's Hospital, and associate professor of medicine at Harvard Medical School. "Early and intensive lipid-lowering with a statin yielded the greatest benefit for acute coronary syndrome patients." In the trial, both drugs were generally well tolerated and discontinuation rates were similar. There were significantly more liver-related side effects with high-dose atorvastatin than with standard-dose pravastatin. Patients who were randomized to atorvastatin 80 mg had a statistically significant, three-fold increase in abnormal elevations of liver function tests compared to patients who were randomized to pravastatin 40 mg (3.3 percent with atorvastatin, and 1.1 percent with pravastatin). "These results were observed in a carefully selected and monitored patient population," said Dr. Cannon. "Physicians must carefully balance safety and efficacy when applying these results to clinical practice." The study also included the investigational use of an antibiotic component marketed by Bristol-Myers Squibb. Since the analysis of this portion of the study is not yet complete, the investigators indicated these results will be presented subsequently. Bristol-Myers Squibb is an established leader in supporting extensive clinical research that has served to explore the benefits of statins, particularly Pravachol � (pravastatin sodium), in a wide variety of patient populations. Pravachol has been the subject of numerous landmark clinical trials including the West of Scotland Coronary Prevention Study (WOSCOPS), the Cholesterol and Recurrent Events (CARE) trial, the Long-term Intervention of Pravastatin in Ischemic Disease (LIPID) trial, and the Prospective Study of Pravastatin in the Elderly at Risk (PROSPER) trial. As a result of groundbreaking research, Pravachol , with diet, has been proven to reduce the risk of first heart attack in patients with elevated cholesterol, when diet and exercise are not enough. Pravachol has also been demonstrated to reduce the risk of a second heart attack or stroke in patients with coronary heart disease, when added to a heart-healthy diet. According to the 2004 update of the American Heart Association's Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics, in 2001, approximately 1.7 million people in the U.S. were discharged from the hospital following an acute coronary syndrome, characterized as a group of clinical conditions that includes heart attack and unstable angina (unpredictable chest pain). The study findings presented today are specific to patients hospitalized for an acute coronary syndrome who are treated within ten days of their event. Without conducting prospective, double-blind randomized clinical trials, the implications for other patient populations cannot be determined. Pravachol (pravastatin sodium), a prescription drug, is not for everyone, including women who are pregnant or nursing or may become pregnant, or people with liver problems. And because serious side effects can result, patients should tell their doctor about any unexplained muscle pain or weakness they experience while on Pravachol , and about any other medications they are taking. Doctors may do blood tests to check for liver problems. It is recommended that liver function tests be performed prior to initiating therapy, prior to increasing the dose, and when otherwise clinically indicated. If a patient develops increased transaminase levels, or signs and symptoms of liver disease, more frequent monitoring may be required, and withdrawal of pravastatin therapy is recommended if an increase in AST or ALT of three times the upper limit of normal or greater persists. Some mild side effects, such as slight rash or stomach upset, occurred in two to four percent of patients.
